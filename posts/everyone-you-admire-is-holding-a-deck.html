Essay — Satire

Everyone You Admire Is Holding a Deck

2026-09-09 · by us · written while wearing clothes that are also real

Everybody says they hate liars. Nobody has ever said it more loudly than the people lying the most.

Think about the lifestyle flex. The rented penthouse for the photo shoot. The rented car for the drive-by. The watch that's real and the life that isn't. There is an entire economy — measurable, documented, prosecuted — built on selling you a life the seller can't fucking afford. In April 2026 a guy named Tyler Bossetti got six years in federal prison for a $20 million Ponzi scheme. What did he spend it on? Rent on a downtown condo, constant travel, a $150,000 Mercedes SUV. He stole twenty million dollars to look like someone who didn't need to steal twenty million goddamn dollars. The fraud wasn't the side quest. The image was the product. The fraud was the fucking funding round.

That's the pattern: the more a life is displayed, the less of it exists. Extravagance isn't a sign of wealth. Extravagance is what a lie looks like when it needs you to stop asking questions.

The AI Century, Same Trick, New Font

You'd think technology would fix lying. More cameras, more records, more receipts. It didn't. It gave lying a better business model.

Albert Saniger founded an app called Nate, told everyone it was AI-powered, raised $40 million on that promise — and behind the curtain, actual humans in the Philippines and Romania were doing the work. The "artificial intelligence" was a guy named Marco with a keyboard. He's charged with fraud now. And here's the part nobody wants to sit with: he didn't invent a new trick. He invented the era's favorite trick — let people believe the machine is magic, never confirm, let the gap fill itself with your imagination. The lie wasn't even spoken. The lie was a goddamn pause.

There's a whole genre of this shit now. Startups that "fooled Silicon Valley" out of nearly half a billion dollars. CEOs with fabricated résumés running $350 million companies. And the response from the industry, every single time, is the same solemn lecture about trust and transparency — delivered by the industry that built the lecture circuit on hype. The same ecosystem that produced "fake it till you make it" as a strategy wants to punish people for faking it. The advice worked exactly as fucking advertised. That's the fuckin crime.

The Confession Economy

Now watch what happens when one of them gets caught. Because the confession is part of the business model too.

The Tate brothers — professional sellers of a rich-guy fantasy — eventually admitted their lavish displays were fake. After selling the lifestyle as proof the lifestyle's advice works. After thousands of dumb-ass kids organized their entire ambition around a rented Lambo. The admission doesn't cost them the audience. The admission is the content. "Everything you see on social media is fake for engagement" gets more engagement than the original lie, because now it's a twist. The con graduated into a reveal show.

Notice the shape of it: confess the lie, keep the audience. The confession rebrands the liar as honest — "at least he tells you it's fake" — which is the most trusted thing a liar can fucking be. It's laundering. You wash the money in prison orange and come out with a documentary deal.

The Deduction Nobody Claims

Meanwhile, the quiet people — the ones with an actual net worth and no merch — are treated like suspects. Nobody screenshots a boring portfolio. There are no highlight reels for paying your goddamn bills. The genuinely rich are, by measurable habit, less visible: no flashy filings, no lifestyle content, no need. Because the truly rich don't perform wealth; they perform modesty, which is just wealth wearing a hoodie. The performance of extravagance is a bill someone expects you to pay. The performance of normal is the luxury.

So the next time someone's life looks like a car commercial, remember what the receipts say. The louder the display, the emptier the tank. The people who seem to be living the most are frequently the ones with the most to hide, because hiding is expensive as hell — a $150,000 SUV, a downtown condo, six years of your life in a federal facility with no windows to post from.

The Punchline Is Us

And the contradiction — how can we claim to hate liars when the most popular people alive are liars? — has a boring-ass answer. We don't hate liars. We hate bad liars. A lie told with confidence is called branding. A lie told with hesitation is called deception. The skill is the difference, not the truth. We reward the polish and punish the stumble, every fucking time, and then we act surprised that the economy of attention optimized for polish.

Nobody gets rich telling you the truth about themselves. The audience shows up for the deck, not the hand. And the house — this time the house is the algorithm — takes its cut from both tables: the one where the liars perform, and the one where we sit watching, knowing, refreshing, half-believing, every goddamn day.

The deck was always marked. The magic isn't that we get fooled. It's that we fucking subscribe.

Real and documented: the Bossetti Ponzi sentencing (April 2026, DOJ SD Ohio), the Nate app fraud charges (DOJ, 2025–26), the "fake AI" startups that fooled investors out of hundreds of millions, the fake-résumé CEO stories, and the Tate brothers' own admission that the displays were staged. The deck is a metaphor. The subscriptions are real.
